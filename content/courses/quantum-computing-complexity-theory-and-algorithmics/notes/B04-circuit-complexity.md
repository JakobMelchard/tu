# B04 Circuit complexity

A Boolean circuit is a straight-line program: no loops, one fixed circuit per input length. Circuit families are a non-uniform model (the circuits for different $n$ need not be related), which makes them both more powerful than Turing machines (they decide some undecidable languages) and more concrete (a circuit is a finite combinatorial object, so lower bounds look like they should be provable). Depth measures parallel time, and the classes NC and AC classify what parallelises. Quantum complexity (B06) is defined with uniform families of quantum circuits, so the uniformity notion here is exactly what is needed there. References: Papadimitriou ch. 4.3 (circuits), 11.4 (P/poly, Karp-Lipton), 15 (parallel computation, NC, P-completeness) [S26]; Karp-Lipton, Barrington, the ~3n lower bound [S56]; class definitions cross-checked against the Complexity Zoo [S34]. The TISS bullet is "circuit complexity classes" [S1]; 192.165 words the same material as looking "inside the class P to study parallelizable problems" [S7]. Exam 1 material.

## Definitions

- **Boolean circuit** on $n$ inputs: a directed acyclic graph with source nodes labelled $x_1, \dots, x_n$ (and constants), internal nodes (gates) labelled $\wedge, \vee$ (fan-in 2 unless stated) or $\neg$ (fan-in 1), unbounded fan-out, and one designated output gate. **Size** = number of gates, **depth** = longest input-to-output path. $C_n$ computes $f : \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$. Every function has a circuit (DNF: size $O(n 2^n)$; Lupanov: $(1 + o(1)) 2^n / n$, and Shannon's counting argument shows most functions need $\Omega(2^n / n)$).
- **Circuit family** $\{C_n\}_{n \ge 0}$ decides $L$ if $C_n(x) = [x \in L]$ for all $|x| = n$. $\mathrm{SIZE}(s(n))$ = languages decided by families with $|C_n| = O(s(n))$.
- **P/poly** $= \bigcup_k \mathrm{SIZE}(n^k)$. Equivalently: languages decided by a polynomial-time TM with a polynomial-length **advice** string $a_n$ depending only on $n$ (advice = description of $C_n$; conversely the TM with advice is converted to a circuit as below).
- **Uniformity.** $\{C_n\}$ is **log-space uniform** if $1^n \mapsto \langle C_n \rangle$ is computable in space $O(\log n)$ (equivalently, the direct connection language "gate $i$ of $C_n$ has type $t$ and inputs $j, k$" is in L); **P-uniform** if computable in time $\mathrm{poly}(n)$. P-uniform polynomial-size families decide exactly P.
- $\mathrm{NC}^k$: log-space uniform families of polynomial size, depth $O(\log^k n)$, fan-in 2. $\mathrm{AC}^k$: same but unbounded fan-in $\wedge, \vee$. $\mathrm{TC}^0$: $\mathrm{AC}^0$ plus unbounded fan-in MAJORITY (threshold) gates. $\mathrm{NC} = \bigcup_k \mathrm{NC}^k = \bigcup_k \mathrm{AC}^k$. Depth is parallel time, size is number of processors.
- **CIRCUIT VALUE (CVP):** given a circuit and an input, compute the output. **P-complete** problems are those in P to which every problem in P log-space reduces.

## Results

**Theorem.** $\mathrm P \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$; in fact the family is log-space uniform.
*Proof (tableau to circuit).* Let $M$ decide $L$ in time $T(n) = n^k$, one tape. The tableau of $M$ on $x$ has $T$ rows and $T$ columns; cell $(i+1, j)$ depends only on cells $(i, j-1), (i, j), (i, j+1)$ through $\delta$, a function of $3 \cdot O(1)$ bits to $O(1)$ bits, hence a constant-size circuit $\mathrm{step}$. $C_n$: row 0 is wired to the input bits (and constants), row $i+1$ is $T$ copies of $\mathrm{step}$ applied to row $i$, the output is an OR over the last row of "cell contains $q_{acc}$". Size $O(T^2) = O(n^{2k})$, depth $O(T)$. The wiring is regular, so $1^n \mapsto \langle C_n \rangle$ is computable in log space (two counters $i, j$). $\square$
The same construction with a random tape gives $\mathrm{BPP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$ once a good random string is fixed (B05), and reversed via Toffoli gates it gives $\mathrm P \subseteq \mathrm{BQP}$ (B06).

**Theorem.** P/poly contains undecidable languages.
*Proof.* Let $U = \{1^n : \text{the } n\text{-th TM halts on the empty input}\}$, undecidable (unary encoding of HALTING). $C_n$ is the constant circuit $1$ or $0$ according to the answer: size 1. The family exists; it is not uniform, and indeed cannot be computed by any algorithm. $\square$ Non-uniformity is the source of this power. It is also why lower bounds are scarce: **no natural uniform class (not even NEXP) is known to lie outside P/poly**. What is known comes from counting: $\mathrm{SIZE}(n^k) \subsetneq \mathrm{SIZE}(n^{k+1})$ for each $k$, and Kannan's theorem $\Sigma_2^p \not\subseteq \mathrm{SIZE}(n^k)$ for each fixed $k$ (which does not exclude $\Sigma_2^p \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$, since $k$ may grow with the language).

**Theorem (Karp-Lipton 1980 [S56]).** If $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$ then $\mathrm{PH} = \Sigma_2^p$.
*Sketch.* Assume SAT has polynomial-size circuits $\{C_n\}$. By self-reducibility (fix $x_1$, ask about the two restrictions, ...) a circuit $C'_n$ of polynomial size **outputs a satisfying assignment** when one exists. Now take a $\Pi_2^p$ language $\{x : \forall y\, \exists z\ \varphi(x, y, z)\}$ with $\varphi$ a formula. It equals $\{x : \exists C'\ \forall y\ \varphi(x, y, C'(\varphi(x, y, \cdot)))\}$: guess the circuit, then for every $y$ check that the circuit's proposed $z$ works; this is a $\Sigma_2^p$ statement (a wrong circuit fails for some $y$). So $\Pi_2^p \subseteq \Sigma_2^p$, hence $\Pi_2^p = \Sigma_2^p$ and PH collapses. $\square$ Interpretation: NP-complete problems probably do not even have non-uniform polynomial algorithms. Contrast B05: BPP $\subseteq$ P/poly is a theorem.

**Circuit lower bounds.** Proving $\mathrm{NP} \not\subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$ would imply $\mathrm P \ne \mathrm{NP}$. The best known lower bound for an explicit function in NP is linear, about $3n$ for fan-in-2 circuits over the full binary basis (precisely $(3+\tfrac1{86})n-o(n)$, Find-Golovnev-Hirsch-Kulikov 2016 [S56]) (Blum 1984: $3n - o(n)$; Find, Golovnev, Hirsch, Kulikov 2016: $(3 + 1/86) n$; Li and Yang 2022: $3.1n$). Why hard: Razborov-Rudich **natural proofs** show that any lower-bound technique that is "constructive and large" (applies to a random function and is efficiently checkable) would break pseudorandom generators, i.e. one-way functions; almost all known combinatorial techniques (switching lemma, approximation method for monotone circuits) are natural. Also relativisation (Baker-Gill-Solovay) and algebrisation barriers. The successes are for restricted classes: $\mathrm{AC}^0$ (below), monotone circuits (Razborov: CLIQUE needs $n^{\Omega(\log n)}$ monotone gates), $\mathrm{ACC}^0$ (Williams 2011: $\mathrm{NEXP} \not\subseteq \mathrm{ACC}^0$).

**Theorem.** $\mathrm{NC}^1 \subseteq \mathrm L \subseteq \mathrm{NL} \subseteq \mathrm{AC}^1 \subseteq \mathrm{NC}^2 \subseteq \cdots \subseteq \mathrm{NC} \subseteq \mathrm P$.
*Proof sketch.* $\mathrm{NC}^1 \subseteq \mathrm L$: evaluate the depth-$O(\log n)$ fan-in-2 circuit by depth-first recursion, storing on the stack one bit ("which child am I in") per level, $O(\log n)$ bits; the gate structure is regenerated by the log-space uniformity machine. $\mathrm{NL} \subseteq \mathrm{AC}^1$: PATH by repeated squaring of the Boolean adjacency matrix with self-loops: $A^{2^{k+1}}_{ij} = \bigvee_l (A^{2^k}_{il} \wedge A^{2^k}_{lj})$, one unbounded-fan-in OR of ANDs per entry, depth $O(1)$ per squaring, $\lceil \log n \rceil$ squarings: depth $O(\log n)$, size $O(n^3 \log n)$; since PATH is NL-complete under log-space (hence $\mathrm{NC}^1$-computable) reductions, all of NL is in $\mathrm{AC}^1$. $\mathrm{AC}^k \subseteq \mathrm{NC}^{k+1}$: an OR of fan-in $m = \mathrm{poly}(n)$ is a binary tree of depth $\log m = O(\log n)$. $\mathrm{NC} \subseteq \mathrm P$: evaluate the polynomial-size circuit gate by gate. $\square$ Below $\mathrm{NC}^1$: $\mathrm{AC}^0 \subsetneq \mathrm{TC}^0 \subseteq \mathrm{NC}^1$, the first inclusion strict by the next theorem.

**Theorem (Furst-Saxe-Sipser, Ajtai 1983; Håstad 1986).** PARITY $\notin \mathrm{AC}^0$. Quantitatively, depth-$d$ circuits for PARITY$_n$ need size $2^{\Omega(n^{1/(d-1)})}$.
*Idea (Håstad's switching lemma).* A **random restriction** $\rho$ fixes each variable independently to 0 or 1 with probability $(1-p)/2$ each and leaves it free with probability $p$. Switching lemma: for a $k$-DNF (or $k$-CNF) $F$, $\Pr_\rho[F|_\rho \text{ has no decision tree of depth} \le s] \le (5 p k)^s$. A decision tree of depth $s$ is both an $s$-CNF and an $s$-DNF, so after a restriction the bottom two layers of a circuit (say OR of ANDs) can be **switched** to AND of ORs and merged with the layer above: the depth drops by one. Apply $d - 2$ rounds with $p \approx n^{-1/(d-1)}$: with positive probability the circuit collapses to a constant-depth-2 formula of small bottom fan-in depending on $n^{1/(d-1)}$ free variables, unless the size is $2^{\Omega(n^{1/(d-1)})}$. But PARITY restricted to any set of free variables is still PARITY (or its negation), which has no decision tree of depth less than the number of free variables. Contradiction. $\square$ Consequence: $\mathrm{AC}^0 \subsetneq \mathrm{TC}^0$ (MAJORITY gates compute PARITY: $x$ has odd weight iff $\bigvee_k [\text{weight} \ge 2k+1] \wedge \neg[\text{weight} \ge 2k+2]$).

**Arithmetic.** ADDITION of two $n$-bit numbers is in $\mathrm{AC}^0$ (carry look-ahead: with generate $g_j = a_j \wedge b_j$ and propagate $p_j = a_j \vee b_j$, carry $c_i = \bigvee_{j < i} \big(g_j \wedge \bigwedge_{j < k < i} p_k\big)$, depth 3 unbounded fan-in), hence in $\mathrm{NC}^1$. MULTIPLICATION is in $\mathrm{TC}^0$ (iterated addition of $n$ partial products, which threshold gates do in constant depth) and in $\mathrm{NC}^1$ (Wallace tree: 3-to-2 carry-save adders reduce $n$ summands to 2 in $O(\log n)$ depth, then one addition), but not in $\mathrm{AC}^0$ (MULTIPLICATION computes PARITY / MAJORITY of a suitably encoded input). Division and iterated multiplication are in $\mathrm{TC}^0$ too (Hesse-Allender-Barrington 2002). Matrix multiplication and determinant are in $\mathrm{NC}^2$; linear algebra parallelises.

**Theorem (Ladner 1975).** CIRCUIT VALUE is P-complete under log-space reductions.
*Proof.* CVP $\in \mathrm P$: topological order, evaluate. Hardness: the tableau-to-circuit construction maps $(M, x)$ with $M$ polynomial-time to a circuit with the input hardwired, in log space. $\square$ Restrictions remain P-complete: monotone CVP, planar CVP; also HORN-SAT, LINEAR PROGRAMMING (feasibility), LEXICOGRAPHICALLY FIRST MAXIMAL INDEPENDENT SET, MAX-FLOW (threshold version). Consequence: CVP $\in \mathrm{NC}$ iff $\mathrm P = \mathrm{NC}$; P-completeness is evidence of *inherent sequentiality*, as NP-completeness is evidence of intractability.

**Parallel computation thesis.** Parallel time on any reasonable parallel model (PRAM, uniform circuits) is polynomially related to sequential space. Precisely: $\mathrm{SPACE}(s) \subseteq$ uniform circuits of depth $O(s^2)$ (Savitch's recursion on the configuration graph, unrolled as a circuit; size $2^{O(s)}$), and a uniform circuit of depth $d \ge \log n$ is evaluated in space $O(d)$ (depth-first, as for $\mathrm{NC}^1 \subseteq \mathrm L$). Hence PSPACE = polynomial parallel time (unbounded processors), and NC = polylogarithmic parallel time with polynomially many processors.

**Bridge to quantum circuits (B06).** A quantum algorithm is by definition a uniform family of quantum circuits over a fixed gate set: without uniformity one could hardwire the answers as with $U$ above, so "polynomial-size quantum circuits exist" is not a complexity statement until one adds that a classical log-space (or polynomial-time) machine outputs the circuit. Classical reversible circuits (Toffoli, fan-out via CNOT) are a special case of quantum circuits, and the tableau-to-circuit construction is how $\mathrm P \subseteq \mathrm{BQP}$ is proved. Conversely the Feynman path sum that gives $\mathrm{BQP} \subseteq \mathrm{PSPACE}$ is a sum over assignments to the wires of the circuit, i.e. circuit-level reasoning again.

## Worked example

*PARITY$_4$ in $\mathrm{NC}^1$.* XOR of two bits: $(a \wedge \neg b) \vee (\neg a \wedge b)$, 3 binary gates plus 2 negations, depth 2 (not counting $\neg$). PARITY$_4 = (x_1 \oplus x_2) \oplus (x_3 \oplus x_4)$: a balanced tree of 3 XORs, depth $2 \cdot 2 = 4$, size $9$ (+ negations). For $n$ bits: depth $2 \lceil \log_2 n \rceil$, size $3(n-1)$: $\mathrm{NC}^1$. A depth-2 circuit (DNF) for PARITY$_n$ needs $2^{n-1}$ terms of size $n$; Håstad says depth 3 needs $2^{\Omega(\sqrt n)}$, so for $n = 100$ the exponent is already of order $\sqrt{100} = 10$, whereas the $\mathrm{NC}^1$ tree uses $297$ gates at depth 14.

*Tableau to circuit, size.* A machine with $T(n) = n^2$ on inputs of length $n = 100$: tableau $10^4 \times 10^4 = 10^8$ cells, each a constant-size step circuit of, say, 50 gates: $5 \cdot 10^9$ gates, depth $10^4$. Polynomial but hopeless in practice; the point is existence and uniformity.

*Carry look-ahead depth.* Adding $n = 8$-bit numbers: $c_7 = \bigvee_{j < 7} (g_j \wedge p_{j+1} \wedge \dots \wedge p_6)$: one layer of ANDs (fan-in up to 7), one OR (fan-in 7), then $s_i = a_i \oplus b_i \oplus c_i$: depth 4 with unbounded fan-in regardless of $n$. The ripple-carry adder has depth $O(n)$: it is the same function, a worse circuit.

*Karp-Lipton self-reducibility.* Given a SAT-deciding circuit $C$ on formulas of length $m$: to find an assignment for $\varphi(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, for $i = 1..n$ ask $C(\varphi|_{x_1 = a_1, \dots, x_{i-1} = a_{i-1}, x_i = 0})$; set $a_i = 0$ if yes else $1$. $n$ calls to $C$ plus formula substitutions: a circuit of size $O(n \cdot |C| + \mathrm{poly}(m))$.

## Pitfalls

- A circuit family is not an algorithm. Without uniformity, "polynomial size" says nothing about computability; with log-space uniformity, polynomial size means exactly P.
- $\mathrm P \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$ but P/poly is not contained in any uniform class (undecidable unary languages). P/poly is a class defined by a *resource on a different model*; "P/poly $\subseteq$ EXPTIME?" is false as stated.
- Depth $\ne$ time. A circuit of depth $d$ and size $s$ runs in parallel time $d$ with $s$ processors, or in sequential time $O(s)$.
- $\mathrm{NC}^1$ vs $\mathrm{AC}^0$: the difference is fan-in. With unbounded fan-in, depth $O(1)$ already computes ADDITION; with fan-in 2, depth $O(1)$ circuits depend on $O(1)$ inputs only.
- PARITY $\notin \mathrm{AC}^0$ is a statement about **constant** depth and polynomial size; PARITY is trivially in $\mathrm{NC}^1$ and in $\mathrm{TC}^0$.
- Lower bounds for specific circuits (DNF size, monotone circuits) do not bound general circuits. Proving $\mathrm{NP} \not\subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$ requires a super-polynomial bound over **all** circuits.
- P-completeness is under log-space (or $\mathrm{NC}^1$) reductions, else meaningless, as with NL-completeness (B03).
- In the quantum setting (B06) uniformity is often stated as polynomial-time uniformity; for BQP the two notions give the same class, since the polynomial-time generator can be absorbed into the circuit via the tableau construction.

## Exam-style questions

1. *Define P/poly in two equivalent ways and prove $\mathrm P \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly}$.* Polynomial-size families / polynomial advice; tableau-to-circuit, size $O(T^2)$.
2. *Give a language in P/poly that is not decidable.* Unary HALTING, constant circuits.
3. *State Karp-Lipton and sketch why the assumption gives circuits that output satisfying assignments.* $\mathrm{NP} \subseteq \mathrm{P/poly} \Rightarrow \mathrm{PH} = \Sigma_2^p$; self-reducibility with $n$ oracle calls.
4. *Prove $\mathrm{NL} \subseteq \mathrm{AC}^1$.* Repeated squaring of the adjacency matrix, $\log n$ layers of depth $O(1)$ with unbounded fan-in; NL-completeness of PATH.
5. *Why is PARITY not in $\mathrm{AC}^0$, and where exactly does the proof use that the function is PARITY?* Random restrictions plus the switching lemma collapse constant-depth small circuits to shallow decision trees; PARITY restricted is still PARITY on the free variables and has decision-tree depth equal to their number.
6. *What does the P-completeness of CIRCUIT VALUE tell you about parallel algorithms?* CVP $\in \mathrm{NC}$ iff $\mathrm{NC} = \mathrm P$; P-complete problems are believed not to parallelise to polylog depth.
